This webinar brings together two practical perspectives on building reliable AI applications. The first presentation introduces a playbook for translating trustworthiness requirements into engineering choices, illustrated through energy market forecasting. The second explores how a graph-based knowledge layer can give AI agents structured access to domain knowledge, processes and memory, using a fictional Minecraft business as a working example.

The Trustworthiness Playbook: From AI Requirements to Engineering Choices

GenAI is quickly moving from experimental tooling into industrial products, workflows and decisions, so the question that matters is no longer whether it works but whether you can trust it. Trust doesn't come from the model alone. It depends on the whole application: its data, its users, how much autonomy it has, what an error costs, and the rules it operates under. Trying to maximise every requirement for every use case only adds complexity and wasted engineering effort.

This presentation introduces the Trustworthiness Playbook, a practical tool that helps teams work out what trust means for their own application. The playbook asks a short set of practical questions about the use case, which reveal the requirements that can't be compromised and point to concrete measures for meeting them.

The session walks through one real-world case step by step: an AI forecasting system whose output is automatically used to place bids on an energy market. It shows how the playbook turns the constraints and limitations of this use case into a small set of clear priorities, and how those priorities become specific engineering choices. Attendees will leave knowing how to assess their own GenAI applications and focus effort where it counts.

Mining for Meaning: A Knowledge Layer for Agents

AI agents are only as reliable as the knowledge they can reach. Stuff it all in the prompt and it gets expensive and stale; let the agent loose on your databases and it guesses, and you can't tell why it said what it did. A knowledge layer is the alternative: a shared, governed graph between your data and your agents, so the agents stay light and the graph does the remembering.

The idea is illustrated by building a knowledge layer for an unlikely company: MineCorp, a fictional business in Minecraft that turns raw materials into higher-value products. An agent supports its workers by answering questions about complex use cases ("what do I need for a diamond pickaxe?", "where do I find diamonds?"). It answers from a graph database holding domain data, step-by-step processes, text from the community wiki and the agent's own memory, through purpose-built, read-only tools.

You will see how to structure the graph (domain, lexical and bridge, with machine-extracted knowledge), how to load and test it (a validated spec checked against the live graph, and evals that catch drift), and what went wrong along the way. Take away a pattern for any domain built on catalogues and processes: runbooks, supply chains, support and operations.
